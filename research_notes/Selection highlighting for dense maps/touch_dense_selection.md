# Touch and mobile selection of small, densely packed targets on a zoomable spatial canvas

Context for the report writer: the target app shows about 110 small light fixtures on a zoomable floor plan (web app, used on phones and tablets). These notes cover platform conventions, HCI evidence on fat-finger and dense-target selection, how selection is shown when the finger covers it, and how map SDKs handle dense or overlapping markers. Numbers from Shift (CHI 2007) and Gunn et al. (CHI 2009) were read from the paper text itself. Other figures come from abstracts, publisher pages or project pages, and are marked as such where it matters.

## Platform conventions: iOS/iPadOS and Android multi-select modes, long-press, drag-select, lasso, design tools, HIG/Material guidance and minimum target sizes

### Takeaway
The platforms share one model for multi-select. You enter an explicit selection mode (a "Select" button on iOS, long-press on Android, or a two-finger pan on iOS lists and grids). In that mode a plain tap toggles an item, a drag sweeps over many items, and a count plus bulk actions appear in a bar. iPad canvas apps like Freeform add a marquee from touch-and-hold on empty canvas, and touch-and-hold one item plus tapping others with a second finger. Minimum touch targets are 44x44 pt on Apple (28x28 pt absolute minimum) and 48x48 dp (about 9 mm) with 8 dp spacing on Android/Material.

### Cited Findings
**iOS / iPadOS**
- In iOS 13 and later, table and collection views support a **two-finger pan multi-select**. When the view recognizes the gesture, the app can put the view into edit mode at once, with no need to tap Edit or Select first. — [Apple: Selecting multiple items with a two-finger pan gesture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/selecting-multiple-items-with-a-two-finger-pan-gesture)
- With the two-finger pan, the selection doesn't have to be contiguous. The user can select a few items, scroll, then pan with two fingers again to add more. Dragging two fingers over items that are already selected deselects them. — [Apple: Selecting multiple items with a two-finger pan gesture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/selecting-multiple-items-with-a-two-finger-pan-gesture)
- Once in edit mode, the user can also select more items by **panning one finger along the axis that doesn't scroll**. For example, a horizontal pan in a vertically scrolling grid selects rather than scrolls. This is how UIKit stops the select gesture from clashing with scrolling. — [Apple: Selecting multiple items with a two-finger pan gesture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/selecting-multiple-items-with-a-two-finger-pan-gesture)
- The app opts in through `shouldBeginMultipleSelectionInteractionAt` / `didBeginMultipleSelectionInteractionAt` / `didEndMultipleSelectionInteraction`. After the pan ends, the view stays in edit mode, so the user can keep selecting with taps until they tap Done. — [Apple: Selecting multiple items with a two-finger pan gesture](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/selecting-multiple-items-with-a-two-finger-pan-gesture)
- **iPhone Photos**: tap "Select" (top right) to enter selection mode. Slide a finger sideways across photos to select them, then move up or down to keep selecting while the grid auto-scrolls. Each selected photo gets a **blue checkmark in its corner**. Share and Delete actions sit in the bottom bar. — [iPhone Life: drag-select multiple photos](https://iphonelife.com/content/tip-day-how-drag-select-multiple-photos); [Trusted Reviews](https://www.trustedreviews.com/how-to/how-to-select-multiple-photos-on-iphone-4426041)
- **iPad Freeform**, two multi-select methods: (1) touch and hold one item, then tap other items with another finger; (2) touch and hold empty canvas, then drag the box that appears around the items (a marquee). The selected group can then be moved, rotated, resized, aligned or grouped. — [Apple Support: Position items on a Freeform board on iPad](https://support.apple.com/en-kw/guide/ipad/ipad0b4f22b0/ipados)
- Apple's iWork apps (Pages, Numbers on iPad/iPhone) have their own "select multiple objects" help pages. I found them but did not open them to confirm the gesture. — [Apple Support: Pages for iPad, select multiple objects](https://support.apple.com/guide/pages-ipad/aside/tanb94f16be3/15.2/ipados/1.0)

**Android / Material**
- Android design guidance since 3.0 makes **long-press the global gesture for selecting data**. A selection Contextual Action Bar (CAB) temporarily covers the app bar while items are selected. — [Android Design: Selection pattern (archived mirror)](https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/sipb/project/android/docs/design/patterns/selection.html)
- After the CAB appears, users add more items with an ordinary tap (no further long-press needed). They leave the mode, clearing the selection, with the system **Back** button or the CAB's checkmark. The actions offered should change with selection size, because single-item actions don't always apply to several items. The guidance says to "reserve the long press gesture for selection exclusively." — [Android Design: Selection pattern (archived mirror)](https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/sipb/project/android/docs/design/patterns/selection.html)
- **Google Photos (Android)**: long-press a photo to start multi-select, then drag across the grid and every photo touched is selected. A recent regression broke this: after the long-press, dragging only scrolled the grid. Users noticed widely and Google shipped a fix. — [Android Authority: drag-to-select missing](https://androidauthority.com/google-photos-drag-to-select-missing-3696148); [Android Authority: fix](https://www.androidauthority.com/google-photos-drag-to-select-fix-3697101/)
- The modern pattern, where a long-press crossfades the top bar to a **selection count** plus contextual actions, is described by a third-party Compose library author as "the Gmail/Photos/Files multi-select pattern that Material 3 never shipped". In other words, M3 may have no official component for it. This is a third-party claim I did not verify against M3 docs. — [compose-contextual-appbar (klibs)](https://klibs.io/project/aldefy/compose-contextual-appbar)

**Canvas tools (tldraw etc.)**
- tldraw's select tool has a `select.brushing` state: a drag on empty canvas draws a selection box. Its ClickManager detects click, double-click and long-press, and a ScribbleManager handles brush and scribble interactions. Release notes say multi-click handling was cut back to double-click only, to improve mobile gestures. — [tldraw docs: editor](https://tldraw.dev/sdk-features/editor); [tldraw release notes v5.2.0](https://tldraw.dev/releases/v5.2.0)

**Minimum target sizes and spacing**
- Apple HIG: the default control size on iOS/iPadOS is **44x44 pt** and the minimum is **28x28 pt**. Spacing matters as much as size: about **12 pt** of padding around bezeled elements and about **24 pt** around elements without a bezel. — [Apple HIG accessibility content (mirror)](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp/blob/b82f0efe2115dc4539c83a2374a714a84aeb350a/content/universal/accessibility.md); [Deque: iOS touch target size rule](https://dequeuniversity.com/rules/attest-ios/1.0/touch-target-size)
- Material Design: touch targets at least **48x48 dp**, separated by **8 dp** or more. The touch target can extend beyond the visual bounds; a 24 dp icon can carry a 48 dp target. 48 dp is about **9 mm** physically, and the recommended size for touchscreen objects is **7–10 mm**. — [Material: Spacing methods](https://material.io/design/layout/spacing-methods.html); [Android Accessibility Help: Touch target size](https://support.google.com/accessibility/android/answer/7101858)

### Inferences
- A floor plan with about 110 fixtures will almost certainly draw fixtures smaller than 44 pt / 48 dp (about 7–10 mm) at the overview zoom. Platform guidance is met by **hit areas larger than the drawn glyph** (Material says so explicitly), not by drawing larger icons. Where hit areas would overlap, some nearest-target rule is needed (see the bubble cursor in the next section).
- Platforms stop selection fighting with scrolling either through an explicit mode (Select button or long-press) or by constraining the axis (UIKit's off-axis pan). On a 2D pannable, pinch-zoomable floor plan, one-finger drag is already pan and two-finger gestures are already pinch. The UIKit two-finger pan doesn't carry over. The Freeform-style **touch-and-hold, then drag a marquee** and the Android-style **long-press to enter mode, then tap to toggle** do carry over.
- The Google Photos regression shows that drag-to-select against scroll/pan is fragile: when the gesture is misrecognized, users notice at once.

### Gaps
- I could not open Apple HIG pages directly (JS-rendered). The 44/28 pt and padding figures come from a mirror of HIG text and Deque, not from apple.com.
- I did not find official Material 3 guidance on selection mode, checkmark overlays or contextual top app bars. The claim that M3 has no official component is unverified.
- I did not verify current selection gestures in Apple Maps, Google Maps, Figma mobile, Miro mobile, Procreate (lasso select) or Apple Notes (lasso with Pencil or finger).
- tldraw's specific long-press behavior on touch (whether long-press on canvas starts a brush select) was not confirmed.

## HCI research on fat-finger and dense-target selection: target sizes, Shift, Offset Cursor, bubble cursor, Escape, TapTap/MagStick, zoom, multi-target selection, lasso vs rectangle

### Takeaway
Bare direct touch fails badly below about 3–10 mm. Shift (CHI 2007) measured up to **81% errors** for 2.6 mm targets, against **4–14%** with an occlusion-avoiding callout or offset cursor. One-handed thumb targets need about **9.2–9.6 mm** (Parhi et al., MobileHCI 2006). The strongest evidence for selecting many targets on a map is Gunn et al. (CHI 2009 extended abstract). In a map task with 14 px targets and distractors, a "Burst" ring of enlarged proxies and a "ZoomTap" zoom-then-tap-to-toggle view both beat Shift by a wide margin: **7%** and **41.9%** errors against **55.9%**, and **5.2 s** and **4.2 s** against **9.5 s**. Holz & Baudisch show much of "fat finger" error is really systematic per-user and per-posture offset.

### Cited Findings
**Target size**
- Parhi, Karlson & Bederson, MobileHCI 2006, "Target Size Study for One-Handed Thumb Use on Small Touchscreen Devices": no significant difference in error rate for targets **≥9.6 mm in discrete tasks** and **≥7.7 mm in serial tasks**. They recommend **9.2 mm (discrete)** and **9.6 mm (serial)**. Speed kept improving as targets grew. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=155150); [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260)
- The paper won the MobileHCI 2016 Best Historical Paper award. — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=155150)

**Shift and Offset Cursor (Vogel & Baudisch, CHI 2007, Best Paper)**
- Shift explains touch failure on small targets as two things together: the finger **occludes the target**, and it is **unclear which part of the finger sets the selection point**. Once a target is smaller than the finger's contact area, users can't tell whether they've acquired it. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Study: 12 participants on an iPAQ PDA, index finger. Square targets of 6, 12, 18, 24, 48 and 96 px, which is **2.6 to 41.9 mm**. Techniques: Touch, Offset Cursor, Shift. Contact: fingertip and fingernail. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- **Unaided touch error rates reached 81% (fingertip) and 63% (fingernail) for the smallest (2.6 mm) targets.** Touch had significantly more errors than Offset and Shift at 6 and 12 px. **Offset and Shift had 4–14% errors at 6 and 12 px**, with no significant difference between them. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Speed: for targets of 18 px and up (fingernail), mean times were Shift 700 ms and Touch 628 ms against **Offset 1027 ms**, a 32% gain for Shift. With the fingertip the crossover was at 48 px: Shift 609 ms, Touch 578 ms, Offset 849 ms, a 28% gain. Shift is about as fast as touch on large targets because it only "escalates" when occlusion is a problem. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- In a pilot, Offset Cursor took **1.57x longer** even for targets big enough for the bare finger. A fixed offset (above the finger) also leaves a strip along the bottom of the screen unreachable, and first-time users aim at the real target and miss. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Escalation rule: the system compares the smallest target under the finger with a learned "occlusion threshold" finger size, and a logistic function turns the difference into a dwell delay. With the threshold fixed at 24 px, the dwell times were **0, 5, 39, 240, 1198 and 1199 ms** for 6 through 96 px targets. Pilots showed many users chose to escalate at 12 px but not at 24 px. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Selection happens on **lift-off** (take-off). The 4–14% residual errors were partly put down to "noisy lift-off": a rolling motion as the finger lifts moves the pointer off a target it had acquired. The authors recommend discarding motion just before take-off. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Shift learns a per-user correction vector between the hardware touch point and the perceived input point: V(t+1) = V(t) + w(P2 − P1), with w = 0.33. Pointer jitter is smoothed by a dynamic recursive low-pass filter: 5 and 18 Hz cut-offs, interpolated between 150 and 560 mm/s. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Basic Shift lets users acquire targets as small as **6 px (2.6 mm)**. For smaller targets, a variant magnifies the callout up to **4x** (and grows it from 26 to 44 mm) and raises the CD ratio. Zoom on a small screen was limited to about 4x. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- Earlier work as summarized in Shift: Potter et al. (CHI 1988) found Offset Cursor with take-off selection on 6.35 mm targets made fewer errors than first-contact or land-on direct touch, but was significantly slower. Albinsson & Zhai (CHI 2003) found that zooming, CD-gain reduction and pixel-step widgets made fewer errors than an unstabilized Offset Cursor for targets **below 0.8 mm**, with zoom the fastest. These were slower than Offset Cursor for targets **above 3.2 mm** because they need several taps even when no adjustment is required. The dual-finger/SimPress work (Benko et al., CHI 2006) found zooming more precise for 1-pixel targets. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF (related work)](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf); [Albinsson & Zhai author page](https://ixdf.org/literature/author/par-anders-albinsson)
- Shift's authors note that target-snapping techniques such as the bubble cursor suit loosely spaced targets, but are "inappropriate when pointing in continuous spaces like a map … where targets are not well defined". — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)

**Bubble cursor**
- Grossman & Balakrishnan, CHI 2005 (Best Paper): an area cursor that resizes its activation area so that **only the single nearest target is ever captured**. In 1D and 2D experiments with varying layout densities it significantly beat the point cursor and object pointing, and its performance fits Fitts' law. — [Grossman & Balakrishnan 2005 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/tgrossman_CHI2005.pdf); [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1054972.1055012)

**Escape (gesture-based disambiguation)**
- Yatani, Partridge, Bern & Newman, CHI 2008: each item is a dot with a "beak" pointing in one of 8 compass directions. The user touches near the item and swipes in the direction shown. **For 6–12 px targets, Escape had a similar error rate to Shift and was at least 30% faster.** — [ACM DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357104); [HCI Museum: Escape](https://hci-museum.lisn.upsaclay.fr/escape)
- Limitation, noted in later work: if a cluster has more targets than distinguishable swipe directions, several targets share a direction and become hard to pick. — [CityU, CHI 2014 paper](https://www.cs.cityu.edu.hk/~rynson/papers/sigchi14a.pdf) (cited from a search summary; I did not open the full text)

**TapTap / MagStick**
- Roudaut, Huot & Lecolinet, AVI 2008, one-handed thumb use on small screens. TapTap: "The first tap defines an area of interest on the screen. The second tap selects the desired target in the popup" (a zoomed copy). TapTap and MagStick were faster than four earlier techniques except Direct Touch, which was faster but more error-prone. TapTap had the lowest error rate and was the one users preferred. — [IP Paris research portal](https://researchportal.ip-paris.fr/en/publications/taptap-and-magstick-improving-one-handed-target-acquisition-on-sm/); [HCI Museum: TapTap](https://hci-museum.lisn.upsaclay.fr/taptap)

**Selecting several targets on a map (the closest match to the lighting use case)**
- Gunn, Zhang, Mak & Irani, CHI 2009 extended abstract, "An Evaluation of One-handed Techniques for Multiple-target Selection". Task: a **prototype map-based program** where users select **four red targets among two distractors**. Three groups of six **14x14 px** square targets, placed at the screen edges or centre. One-handed thumb use; 18 participants, 1620 trials. — [Gunn et al. 2009 PDF](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/2009-CHI-Gunn-OneHandedMultipleTargetTechniques.pdf)
  - **ZoomTap** (based on TapTap): tap an area to open a zoomed view, **tap targets inside it to toggle them**, flick to move the zoom focus (shown by a red square on the background), and tap outside to close.
  - **Burst** (based on Shift, Escape, StarBurst and circling): press the thumb, and the system picks the **six nearest selectable targets** and draws **enlarged proxy copies in a ring around the finger**. Sliding over a proxy selects it, so no cursor aiming is needed.
  - Their Shift: a 65 px callout, offset 65 px upward, with a 5-sample rolling average.
  - **Completion time**: ZoomTap **4235 ms**, Burst **5248 ms**, Shift **9546 ms**. All differences significant.
  - **Error rate**: Burst **7%**, ZoomTap **41.9%**, Shift **55.9%**. All differences significant. An error was a distractor selected, or a lift with nothing selected.
  - **Preference**: ZoomTap > Burst > Shift. Shift lost because it selects one target at a time and edge targets were hard to reach.
  - The authors note that their Shift build did not fully match the original (filter and callout placement), so Shift's numbers may be pessimistic. — [Gunn et al. 2009 PDF](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/2009-CHI-Gunn-OneHandedMultipleTargetTechniques.pdf)

**What "fat finger" error really is (Holz & Baudisch)**
- Holz & Baudisch, CHI 2010, "The Generalized Perceived Input Point Model": their model "explained 67% of the touch inaccuracy that was previously attributed to the fat finger problem". Touch inaccuracy is "primarily the result of failure to distinguish between different users and finger postures, rather than the fat finger problem". The fingerprint-based RidgePad was **1.8x** more accurate than simulated capacitive sensing, and an optical-tracking prototype **3.3x** (they suggest up to 3.3² > 10x more controls in the same area). — [SIPLab project page](https://siplab.org/projects/Generalized_Perceived_Input_Point_Model); [Christian Holz](https://www.christianholz.net/generalized_perceived_input_point_model)
- Holz & Baudisch, CHI 2011, "Understanding Touch": users aim using visual features on top of the finger, not the centre of the contact area. A "projected center" model had **1.6 mm** error against **4 mm** for the traditional contact-area model, about 60% less. — [SIPLab: Understanding Touch](https://siplab.org/projects/Understanding_Touch)

**Lasso, rectangle and tapping for group selection**
- Dehmeshki & Stuerzlinger (SFU, mouse-based): standard lasso and rectangle group selection "can be time consuming and error prone", especially for objects along curved paths in dense areas. Their perceptual (Gestalt-based) group selection beat rectangle and lasso when targets had perceptual structure, and was competitive for arbitrary layouts. — [SFU: Design and Evaluation of a Perceptual-based Object Group Selection Technique](https://vvise.iat.sfu.ca/pubs/dehmeshki2010designeval)
- A search summary also states that lasso suits complex-shaped groups with many items, that tapping each item is faster for small groups, and that a rectangle plus a few extra taps can be efficient. I could not tie this to a specific primary source, so treat it as unverified. — [LY Corp research publication 398](https://research.lycorp.co.jp/en/publications/398) (attribution uncertain)
- Mizobuchi & Yasumura (CHI 2004) compared tapping and circling for pen selection. I found the paper but did not read its results. — [CHI 2004 PDF](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/materials/conferences/CHI2004/1p607.pdf)

### Inferences
- At typical phone overview zooms, a floor plan with about 110 fixtures will have fixture glyphs and gaps in the 2–6 mm range, right where Shift measured 63–81% bare-touch error. Without help (larger snap-to-nearest hit areas, zoom, or a disambiguation step), tap-to-select on the overview will be unreliable.
- Of the techniques found, Gunn et al.'s map task is the closest analogue: toggle-selecting several small targets among distractors on a map. Both winners avoid precise aiming. **Burst** replaces the dense neighbourhood with an enlarged ring of proxies (7% errors). **ZoomTap** magnifies, then lets taps toggle items (fastest at 4.2 s). A zoomable floor plan already offers "ZoomTap for free" through pinch zoom. A Burst-style ring of the nearest fixtures after a long-press is a cheap extra for ambiguous taps.
- The bubble cursor's nearest-target capture, implemented as tap-resolves-to-nearest-fixture within a radius, i.e. Voronoi hit areas, gives each fixture the largest possible hit area at any zoom. Shift's authors flag that it fits poorly where targets are not well defined. Fixtures are discrete, well-defined targets, so the objection doesn't apply here. Nearest-target capture would still need a maximum radius, so taps on empty floor can still deselect or pan.
- Holz & Baudisch's finding that error is systematic per user and per posture suggests modest fixed corrections, such as interpreting a touch slightly above the contact centroid, help. Even so, size or disambiguation still dominate.
- Shift's lift-off rule, plus discarding the last few ms of motion before lift-off, is directly usable for any press-drag-release selection (callout, ring or marquee).

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative study of **lasso vs rectangle marquee on finger touch** for dense spatial targets. The evidence is mouse- or pen-based.
- I couldn't get MagStick's mechanism or its own numbers. The sources describe it only as the companion to TapTap.
- I didn't find touch adaptations of the bubble cursor with numbers (e.g. touch "area cursor" or "LinearDragger").
- Holz & Baudisch's minimum button sizes for 95% accuracy appear only in figures. The mm values weren't in the text I could access.
- Gunn et al. is a short extended abstract on a 2009 device; its absolute times and error rates may not carry over to modern capacitive phones.

## How selection state is shown under the finger: callouts, magnifiers, proxies, haptics, checkmarks and counts

### Takeaway
Research handles occlusion by showing a copy of what's under the finger somewhere the hand doesn't cover. Shift uses a 26 mm round callout, placed above by default and repositioned at screen edges, with a dashed leader to the contact point. Burst uses a ring of enlarged proxies. Shipping apps show selection state on each item (a checkmark badge) and in total (a count plus bulk actions in a top or bottom bar). Haptics mark discrete selection changes on iOS.

### Cited Findings
- **Shift callout design**: the first prototype moved the *whole display* up by 22 mm on contact, but "shifting too much screen space can disorient users". The final design is a **circular callout, 26 mm in diameter**, showing a copy of the occluded area. Its round shape stands out from rectangular layouts, so extra highlighting proved unnecessary. A **dashed line** joins the shifted pointer to the real contact point. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- **Placement**: by default the callout sits above the target. Near the left or right edge it moves toward the middle. Near the top edge it goes to the left, or to the right if that isn't possible. This can be mirrored for left-handed users. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- **Users relied on the callout for feedback**: participants "always used the callout for visual feedback, even if they realized that the target in the main display was visible". In the study, targets also turned red when acquired. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- When magnified (zoom variant), the callout grew from 26 to 44 mm and **tracks the finger like a tracking menu** if the contact point moves past a threshold, so the callout itself doesn't end up under the finger. — [Vogel & Baudisch 2007 PDF](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/papers/dvogel_CHI2007.pdf)
- **Burst** solves occlusion by "displaying occluded targets in an offset location" as **enlarged proxy copies in a ring around the finger**. Selection is by sliding onto a proxy, not by aiming a cursor. — [Gunn et al. 2009 PDF](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/2009-CHI-Gunn-OneHandedMultipleTargetTechniques.pdf)
- **ZoomTap** marks the magnified region with a **red square on the background map**, so users keep context while toggling targets in the zoomed view. — [Gunn et al. 2009 PDF](https://hci.cs.umanitoba.ca/assets/publication_files/2009-CHI-Gunn-OneHandedMultipleTargetTechniques.pdf)
- **TapTap** shows a zoomed popup of the tapped area for the second, selecting tap. — [HCI Museum: TapTap](https://hci-museum.lisn.upsaclay.fr/taptap)
- **Per-item marker**: iPhone Photos puts a blue checkmark in the corner of each selected photo, during drag-select as well. — [iPhone Life](https://iphonelife.com/content/tip-day-how-drag-select-multiple-photos); [Trusted Reviews](https://www.trustedreviews.com/how-to/how-to-select-multiple-photos-on-iphone-4426041)
- **Aggregate state**: Android's selection CAB covers the app bar while anything is selected. Its actions change with the number of items, and Back or the checkmark exits and clears. — [Android Design: Selection pattern (archived)](https://stuff.mit.edu/afs/sipb/project/android/docs/design/patterns/selection.html). The modern Gmail/Photos/Files version crossfades the top bar to a **selection count** with contextual actions. — [compose-contextual-appbar (klibs)](https://klibs.io/project/aldefy/compose-contextual-appbar)
- **Haptics**: on iOS, `UISelectionFeedbackGenerator` "indicate[s] a change in selection" and conveys movement through a series of discrete values, as in pickers. Standard controls (switches, sliders, pickers) play system haptics automatically, and the HIG says to use feedback generators where it makes sense. — [Apple HIG: Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics); [Apple: UISelectionFeedbackGenerator](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uiselectionfeedbackgenerator)

### Inferences
- For fixtures smaller than a fingertip, a checkmark drawn *on* the fixture is hidden while the finger is down and is too small to read afterwards. The research and platform patterns point to three channels together: (1) a live, unoccluded preview during the press, like Shift's callout or Burst's ring, or simply the marquee outline extending past the finger; (2) a persistent mark on each fixture that stays visible at overview zoom, such as a ring or halo bigger than the glyph; (3) a count chip or contextual bar ("12 selected" plus actions, clear, done) in a fixed screen location, which is the most reliable confirmation on a small screen.
- Shift's finding that users trusted the callout even when the target was visible suggests that a magnified callout or loupe during long-press or marquee drags is worth the space on phones, even when the user could have seen the target.
- A per-fixture haptic tick, like iOS selection feedback, would match native conventions during drag-selection. Whether a web app can trigger haptics depends on browser support (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- I found no quantitative comparison of selection-highlight styles (halo vs fill vs checkmark vs outline) for tiny targets on touch screens.
- I did not research web haptics (Vibration API support on iOS Safari vs Android Chrome) in this pass. This matters because the app is a web app.
- I didn't verify how Apple Maps or Google Maps show the selected pin (enlarged pin, bottom sheet).

## Mobile map conventions for overlapping and dense markers: clustering, zoom-on-tap, spiderfying and disambiguation

### Takeaway
Map SDKs stack three strategies. **Clustering** replaces dense groups with a count bubble when zoomed out. **Tapping a cluster zooms in** to the level where it splits (Google `zoomOnClick` / `fitBounds`; Mapbox `getClusterExpansionZoom` + `easeTo`). **Spiderfying** fans markers that still overlap into a circle (fewer than 9) or a spiral (9 or more), with legs back to their true positions. That covers markers at max zoom or on the same spot.

### Cited Findings
- **Google Maps clustering**: when a cluster is clicked, the default is to zoom into it by calling `fitBounds()` on the cluster's bounds. `zoomOnClick` defaults to true and can be turned off. — [react-google-maps-api MarkerClusterer docs](https://www.mintlify.com/JustFly1984/react-google-maps-api/api/marker-clusterer)
- The Google Maps Android utility groups markers into clusters by zoom level, through a `ClusterManager` and a customizable `ClusterRenderer`. — [Google Maps Android Marker Clustering Utility](https://developers.google.com/maps/documentation/android-sdk/utility/marker-clustering); [iOS utility](https://developers.google.cn/maps/documentation/ios-sdk/utility/marker-clustering)
- **Mapbox GL JS / MapLibre**: on a cluster click, `getClusterExpansionZoom(clusterId)` returns the zoom at which the cluster splits, and the map `easeTo`s that zoom centred on the cluster, showing the child points or sub-clusters. — [Mapbox: Create and style clusters](https://docs.mapbox.com/mapbox-gl-js/example/cluster/); [MapLibre example](https://maplibre.org/maplibre-gl-js/docs/examples/create-and-style-clusters/)
- **Leaflet.markercluster** "spiderfies" a cluster into a fan of individual pins once markers can't be separated further. `spiderfyOnMaxZoom` turns this on at the bottom zoom level. `spiderfyDistanceMultiplier` spreads the fan further. It fires `spiderfied` / `unspiderfied` events. — [Leaflet.markercluster (yarn)](https://classic.yarnpkg.com/en/package/leaflet.markercluster); [ipyleaflet MarkerCluster docs](https://ipyleaflet.readthedocs.io/en/latest/layers/marker_cluster.html)
- **OverlappingMarkerSpiderfier (Leaflet)**: exists because overlapping markers can't be clicked. Unlike clustering, it handles markers at identical or nearly identical spots. Markers within **`nearbyDistance` = 20 px** spiderfy together. They form a **circle below `circleSpiralSwitchover` = 9** markers and a **spiral** above it, with **legs** (`legWeight` 1.5) to their true positions. By default (`keepSpiderfied: false`), clicking a spidered marker collapses the group before the click is handled. `keepSpiderfied: true` keeps the fan open for clicking several in a row. — [OverlappingMarkerSpiderfier-Leaflet](https://github.com/MITREDan/OverlappingMarkerSpiderfier-Leaflet)

### Inferences
- Floor-plan fixtures have fixed, known positions and a modest count (about 110). Full clustering, which hides individual fixtures, may cost more than it gains, because users want to see each light. The **tap-ambiguous-area-to-zoom** behaviour (Mapbox/Google) and **spiderfy-on-ambiguity** (OMS) both transfer: a tap that lands between several fixtures can either zoom to separate them or fan them out, as Burst does.
- OMS's `keepSpiderfied` option is a ready precedent for multi-select. Leaving the fan open after a pick supports toggling several neighbours in a row, which matches Gunn et al.'s finding that one-target-at-a-time techniques (Shift) were much slower for multi-target tasks.
- The OMS 20 px "nearby" radius, the 9-item circle/spiral switch and Burst's six nearest targets are concrete, tested-in-practice defaults to start from.

### Gaps
- I didn't confirm how consumer map apps (Apple Maps, Google Maps app) resolve taps on overlapping POIs, for example with a disambiguation list or bottom sheet. Only SDK behaviour is documented here.
- I found no HCI study comparing spiderfying, clustering and disambiguation menus on touch.
